ME2502 Reinforcement Learning — Final Project Proposal
Abstract
Pretrained chemical language models provide useful molecular priors, but ordi-
nary sampling does not directly optimize a desired property proﬁle. We propose a
controlled study of whether reinforcement learning improves target-directed gen-
eration under a limited evaluation budget, and how a ﬁxed-prior Kullback–Leibler
(KL) penalty changes the trade-off between optimization and output quality. Start-
ing from public ChemGPT weights, we will compare frozen sampling and selec-
tion, REINFORCE, and KL-regularized REINFORCE on two GuacaMol multi-
property tasks, using three seeds and matched unique-molecule budgets. Evalu-
ation will distinguish candidate discovery from the ﬁnal policy’s routine outputs,
while tracking validity, diversity, repetitions, and actual computation. The project
will deliver a reproducible implementation and an analysis of both improvements
and failure modes. In particular, we will examine whether better search scores cor-
respond to a broadly improved output distribution or to increasing concentration
on a small set of candidates.

Background and related work
Molecular generation proposes structures for de novo design, lead exploration, and property opti-
mization. Learning a plausible chemical distribution and ﬁnding molecules that satisfy a speciﬁed
objective are related but distinct tasks. Useful evaluation therefore includes property quality, struc-
tural coverage, and the number of candidates that must be assessed.
A candidate may be similar to a reference structure yet miss a desired physicochemical proﬁle, while
a high aggregate reward can conceal a weak component. We therefore consider the joint behavior of
the component properties and the production of multiple distinct candidates. This makes molecular
optimization a useful setting for studying how feedback changes a pretrained generator under ﬁnite
evaluation resources.
Existing generators span several representations and mechanisms. Sequence models learn SMILES
or SELFIES autoregressively; ChemGPT extends this approach through chemical language pretrain-
ing [1]. Graph and fragment models represent connectivity explicitly: JT-VAE combines chemical
substructures with graph message passing [2], and MolGAN uses adversarial graph generation with
a property-oriented learning objective [3]. Normalizing ﬂows such as MoFlow learn invertible graph
representations [4], while equivariant diffusion models generate atom types and three-dimensional
coordinates [5]. Representation, generator architecture, and goal-directed optimization are separate
design choices that can be combined.
Goal-directed methods include conditional generation, sampling and ﬁltering, latent-space optimiza-
tion, genetic search, and reinforcement learning (RL). Graph-based genetic algorithms illustrate
that relatively simple search can be competitive on some objectives [6]. REINVENT established a
chemical-prior-plus-feedback route [7]; ChemRLformer further examined language representations


and RL design choices [8]. GuacaMol supplies standardized molecular design tasks [9], and the
Practical Molecular Optimization (PMO) benchmark emphasizes oracle-call efﬁciency [10].
Our project builds on this established literature by integrating and evaluating on-policy RL on a ﬁxed
public ChemGPT prior. Keeping the generator and scoring deﬁnitions ﬁxed allows us to isolate the
effects of parameter updates and prior regularization. We ask: RQ1, does RL improve discov-
ery relative to equal-budget frozen sampling and selection? RQ2, does KL regularization preserve
useful output characteristics, and at what cost to the optimization score? The study will connect
policy-gradient learning and prior regularization to an explicit molecular environment, producing a
controlled comparison that separates the beneﬁts of learning from those of repeated sampling.

Proposed method
Model and environment.
We will use the public ncfrey/ChemGPT-4.7M checkpoint1 and its
matched SELFIES/WordPiece tokenizer, without pretraining from scratch. The state is the current
token preﬁx and time step; an action selects a token or terminates generation. Each completed
molecule receives a deterministic task reward R ∈[0, 1]. Empty, undecodable, disconnected, or
length-truncated outputs receive zero. The horizon is 256 sampled actions. All methods share
the same checkpoint-compatible start/end tokens, ﬁxed special-token mask, and temperature-one
categorical sampling distribution.
A ﬁxed processing chain will convert sampled tokens into SELFIES, decode them to SMILES,
and apply RDKit validity checks. The resulting canonical identity will index the score cache and
candidate collection. We will retain the original token sequence, termination status, and property
components alongside each evaluation so that failures and repeated proposals remain visible.

Experimental design
Tasks and controls.
We will use GuacaMol’s goal-directed v2 deﬁnitions, whose selected MPO
rewards use geometric aggregation. Table 1 separates development from the two main comparisons.
Scores combine reference similarity and calculated physicochemical properties; they are proxies for
design requirements, not measured binding or efﬁcacy.

Fexofenadine MPO favors reference similarity and sufﬁciently high TPSA while limiting logP. Os-
imertinib MPO combines similarity requirements from two ﬁngerprints with a different physico-
chemical proﬁle. Their components will be scored using the published deﬁnitions, with no task-
speciﬁc rescaling from main-run outcomes. Each task will be optimized independently from the
same initial checkpoint.
The three conditions are Frozen + best-of-B, retaining the best observed candidates without param-
eter updates; REINFORCE, using β = 0; and REINFORCE + KL, using the ﬁxed-prior penalty.
Two tasks, three conditions, and three seeds yield 18 runs. Development will use Perindopril and
separate seeds. Fexofenadine has already been explored in an earlier, different GRU pilot; this his-
tory will be disclosed rather than presented as a fresh blind test. Main-task outcomes will not select
hyperparameters, seeds, or checkpoints. For each search curve, frozen and trainable methods will be
compared using the same top-k statistic over candidates available at that point. The frozen baseline
will receive the full evaluation budget under the same sampling protocol.
Evaluation budget.
Each run may score up to B = 3,000 previously unseen valid canonical
molecules, with additional caps of 15,000 generation attempts, 470 optimizer updates, and 30 min-
utes of search/training time. The ﬁrst exhausted limit ends the run. The tail batch size is determined
before sampling by remaining unique-query capacity. Invalid and repeated attempts are recorded;
caching never grants another run free logical queries. Unique-query matching does not imply equal
training computation, so updates, tokens, physical scoring calls, and wall time will also be reported.
The evaluation ledger will distinguish generation attempts, ﬁrst-time valid identities, cache hits, and
actual scoring calls. This will reveal whether a method spends substantial computation revisiting
known candidates even when its unique-query count appears efﬁcient.
Outcomes and analysis.
The primary endpoint is the normalized area under the top-10 MPO
score curve against new unique evaluations. We will also report budget-end scores, the number of
threshold-passing candidates, and strict joint component satisfaction; an aggregate MPO threshold
does not imply that every component passes. For early-stopped runs, ﬁxed-budget summaries will
explicitly identify any last-value carry-forward and show the achieved query count.
Each terminal policy will independently generate 1,024 additional attempts, without further training
or checkpoint selection. We will measure all-attempt mean reward, validity, unique valid output, rep-
etition, and ﬁngerprint/scaffold diversity. Search histories and ﬁnal-policy samples will be analyzed
separately. Results will show every seed, means and variability, and paired differences for each task.
A higher search score accompanied by collapsed routine outputs, or a KL model trading peak score
for diversity, will be analyzed as a trade-off. We will inspect component-score and sequence-length
distributions alongside KL and repetition curves to explain these outcomes. Planned ﬁgures will
include budget–quality curves, paired method comparisons, and a summary of the quality–diversity
trade-off.

Feasibility, resources, and work plan
Feasibility and resources.
Initial CPU checks loaded the public ChemGPT checkpoint, generated
32 naturally terminated and decodable single-molecule samples, veriﬁed rollout/teacher-forcing log-
probability agreement, and obtained ﬁnite nonzero gradients. An enumerable two-step environment
checked the KL score-function and leave-one-out gradient construction. H100 resource proﬁling has
also been completed. These are feasibility checks; the formal 18-run comparison remains planned.
The next development milestone is an end-to-end short run that validates the scorer, query ledger,
and frozen-reference behavior together. A small development conﬁguration will then be ﬁxed and
reused across both main tasks and all reported seeds. The planned computing resources are one
NVIDIA H100 80GB GPU, 4 vCPUs, 64 GiB of host RAM, and 10 GB of project storage.

interfaces for integration with the RL framework; and coordinate the proposal’s background, moti-
vation, and overall narrative in the ﬁnal report. This work will provide the tested model interface
and the study’s scientiﬁc framing.
Liao Yang will develop the GuacaMol task, property-scoring, and reward interfaces; implement
